\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Financial transaction ecosystems have undergone massive digitization, leading to exponential transaction volumes alongside increasingly sophisticated, adversarial fraudulent activity \cite{leborgne2022handbook}. In retail banking and merchant acquiring networks, fraudulent credit card transactions result in tens of billions of dollars in annual global losses, severe merchant chargeback penalties, and diminished consumer confidence \cite{hand2001prospecting, carcillo2018scarff}. 

The design of automated machine learning systems for fraud detection operates under severe operational constraints that challenge standard supervised learning assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Class Imbalance:} Fraudulent events represent an ultra-rare class, typically occurring at base rates well below $0.5\%$ \cite{dalpozzolo2015calibrating}. In the benchmark European credit card stream analyzed in this work \cite{dalpozzolo2018credit}, only $492$ out of $284,807$ transactions ($0.1727\%$) are fraudulent. Standard classification objectives (e.g., cross-entropy minimization or misclassification error) collapse toward predicting the majority class, achieving $99.83\%$ superficial accuracy while missing all financial compromises.
    \item \textbf{Verification Lag and Asymmetric Feedback:} True labels are not immediately observable at the point of sale. While non-fraudulent transactions are passively accepted without confirmation, fraudulent behavior is identified only days or weeks later following cardholder complaints or formal bank chargeback dispute resolutions \cite{leborgne2022handbook}.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Non-Stationarity and Concept Drift:} Fraud syndicates continually alter their spending amounts, timing patterns, and merchant targets to circumvent static thresholding rules. Simultaneously, normal consumer spending habits evolve over seasonal cycles and macroeconomic fluctuations, inducing continuous temporal distribution drift \cite{carcillo2018scarff}.
\end{enumerate}

To circumvent the vulnerability of fully supervised pipelines to label scarcity and feedback delays, fraud detection can be framed as an \emph{unsupervised anomaly detection} problem. Under this paradigm, a statistical model estimates the probability density function $p(\mathbf{x})$ or bounding envelope of legitimate cardholder behavior using non-fraudulent historical transactions. Unseen transactions exhibiting low likelihood under the learned normal density are flagged for human triage.

\subsection{Scope of Phase 1 and Project Contributions}
In Phase~1 of Project T033, we investigate statistical and classical machine learning approaches for credit card fraud triage, focusing on parametric density modeling via Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) estimated using Expectation-Maximization (EM) \cite{dempster1977maximum}. We benchmark GMM against established anomaly detection baselines: Isolation Forest \cite{liu2008isolation}, One-Class Support Vector Machines (OC-SVM) \cite{scholkopf2001estimating}, and PCA Reconstruction Error \cite{shyu2003novel}. Deep learning formulations (such as deep autoencoders) are reserved for Phase~2 as planned future work.

The principal contributions of this work are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict Chronological Validation Protocol:} We establish a non-overlapping, sequential time split (60\% train, 20\% validation, 20\% test) spanning 48 hours of transactions. All feature standardizations are fit strictly on training-legitimate transactions to completely prevent data leakage across time and class boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Distributional and Gaussianity Auditing:} We perform comprehensive statistical testing (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Shapiro-Wilk, skewness, and kurtosis) on all numerical feature dimensions to empirically test the core multivariate normality assumption underlying GMMs in transaction spaces.
    \item \textbf{Operational Capacity-Constrained Evaluation:} Moving beyond misleading ROC-AUC metrics, we evaluate models using Precision-Recall curves (PR-AUC) and operational alert capacity metrics (Precision@$k$ and Recall@$k$ for triage budgets $k \in \{50, 100, 200\}$).
    \item \textbf{Systematic Model Selection:} We conduct an empirical comparison of GMM covariance topologies (spherical, diagonal, tied, and full) across component counts $K \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 10\}$, governed by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) \cite{akaike1974new} and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) \cite{schwarz1978estimating}.
\end{itemize}
